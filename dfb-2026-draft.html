The draft, reviewed

Nobody won this draft on value

The best roster in the league belongs to the manager who reached hardest all night. So does the eleventh-place one. What separated them was never how far they reached — only what they reached for.

By The War Room Sat Sept 5, 2026 · 2:32 PM ET Slot 3 · Rage

The snake ran clean from Jahmyr Gibbs at 1.01 to Dominic Zvada at 15.12, and when it stopped it had produced a result that ought to bother anyone who drafts with a ranking list in their lap.

Two managers ignored their boards more than anyone else in this league. One of them built the best roster in it. The other finished eleventh. That is the finding, and it is not the one anybody expects: how far you reach predicts nothing at all. Across all twelve teams the correlation between average draft value and actual roster strength is 0.04 — which is to say value explains roughly one tenth of one percent of who drafted well.

Honey Bijan's Mustard finished with the second-worst average value in the league — his picks came, on average, eight slots ahead of where consensus said they belonged. He also finished with a starting lineup worth 543 points above replacement, a hundred and fifty clear of second place. Both things are true. The second one is the one that wins games.

He got there by buying the positions nobody else would pay for. Lamar Jackson is the second-best quarterback rostered. Trey McBride is the best tight end. Brandon Aubrey is the best kicker. Those three slots — the ones this league treated as chores — returned him 270 points on their own. Everything he overpaid for, he overpaid for on purpose.

Round one, and the reversal

Eight backs, then a change of heart

The room opened on running backs and opened hard: eight of the first twelve picks, five of the first seven. Then it simply stopped. Round two went 5–6 to receivers, round three went 3–7, and across the twelve rounds that followed, running back led outright exactly twice.

By the final horn the league had taken 68 receivers to 54 backs. This is a room that talked itself into running-back scarcity for eleven minutes and then spent four hours quietly acting on the opposite belief.

Cumulative players drafted, by position, through each round. Receivers overtake backs in round three and never trail again.

Quarterbacks arrived in two distinct waves — three in the sixth and four in the eighth, then a second stampede of four in the thirteenth and three in the fourteenth. Ten of the twelve managers waited past pick 60 for their first quarterback, and the patience paid: four of the ten biggest bargains of the entire draft were quarterbacks taken after pick 150.

Honey Bijan's Mustard

Goudariddance · slot 4 · VOR 543 · depth 10

Puka Nacua at 1.04 and Ashton Jeanty at 2.04 is the strongest opening pair anybody drafted, and it is still not why he won. That would be Jackson, McBride and Aubrey — the best or near-best asset at three positions his rivals filled with leftovers.

The vulnerability is real. Receiver is thin behind Nacua, where his WR2 slot produces almost nothing, and starting two tight ends means a single McBride injury collapses two lineup spots at once. His bench offers no receiving help at all.

Rage

Gooon · slot 3 · VOR 392 · depth 21 · no defense

The best receiving corps in the league top to bottom. Ja'Marr Chase is the single most valuable starter anybody rostered, and behind him sit the 7th, 11th and 24th receivers on the board — with the 34th on the bench. Drafting from the turn, he took two every time and it shows.

The draft's best single pick was his: MarShawn Lloyd in the ninth, taken 33 picks ahead of the room's average draft position, after a research pass three days earlier caught that Lloyd had inherited Green Bay's starting job. That same pass struck Josh Jacobs from the board — a player another manager spent a sixth-rounder on.

Two problems. The backfield is six deep and one starter tall: plenty of bodies, no genuine RB2. And there is no defense on the roster, which is a mandatory starting slot and a waiver chore before Week 1.

Waiver wire

What is still out there

Thirty-five ranked players went undrafted, and they cluster hard by position in a way that says something about how this room drafts.

Quarterback is fully stocked. Jordan Love, C.J. Stroud, Cam Ward and Sam Darnold are all unowned. So is tight end — T.J. Hockenson, Brenton Strange, Chig Okonkwo and Oronde Gadsden II are there for anyone wanting an upgrade. At receiver: Adonai Mitchell, Jalen McMillan, Jauan Jennings.

There is no running back left. Not one of consequence. Fifty-four went off the board and the room cleaned out every back with a plausible path to touches. Whatever backfield help anyone needs this season arrives by trade or by injury, not by waiver claim.

And three teams need a defense before Thursday.

How the grades were calculated

Grades measure starting-lineup value over replacement, not adherence to consensus. Every drafted player maps to his rank within his own position on a 194-player post-cutdown board; that positional rank converts to a full-PPR season projection through a fitted decay curve. Replacement level is set for this exact format — twelve teams, one quarterback, two flex — at QB14, RB34, WR40, TE14, K13 and DEF13.

Each roster's best legal lineup is then solved and the ten starters' value above replacement summed. An unfilled mandatory slot costs 30 points. Bench depth counts the best backup at each position above replacement at a quarter weight, and stacking more than three starters on one bye week carries a penalty.

  • Known limits. The projections are a curve fitted to positional rank, not real projections. Treat the tiers as meaningful and the ordering inside a tier as noise.
  • Ties are ties. Under six different scoring assumptions, places 3–5 and 8–10 reorder freely. First and last never moved. Rage falls to fifth if an empty defense is priced harder than the 30 points charged here.
  • Kickers and defenses dominate any "biggest reach" list mechanically, and were discounted accordingly in the write-ups.

Source data is the completed Sleeper draft, refetched and verified against the archive: 180 picks, fifteen per team, no duplicates. Roster holes were confirmed against Sleeper's own position data rather than inferred from the board.
